Imagine running a spam filter on a batch of 100 incoming emails. In reality, 40 of them are actual spam (positive) and 60 are legitimate emails (negative).

When we evaluate the filter's predictions against the actual labels, we get:

| | Predicted Positive | Predicted Negative | Total |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Actual Positive** | 30 (TP) | 10 (FN) | **40** |
| **Actual Negative** | 5 (FP) | 55 (TN) | **60** |
| **Total** | **35** | **65** | **100** |

The confusion usually comes from mixing up which total you divide by. It helps to look at it from two different perspectives:

## 1. Precision — The model perspective

Look at the model's output: it flagged 35 emails as spam. But when we inspect those 35 predictions, only 30 were genuinely spam, while 5 were legitimate emails that got wrongly caught in the filter.

```
Precision = 30 / 35 = 0.857 = 85.7%
```

Out of everything the model claimed was positive, how many were actually right?

## 2. Recall — The dataset perspective

Now look at the dataset itself: there were 40 actual spam emails in total. The filter caught 30 of them and missed 10.

```
Recall = 30 / 40 = 0.75 = 75%
```

Out of everything that was actually positive in reality, how many did the model find?

TPR (True Positive Rate) is exactly the same as Recall: `TPR = TP / (TP + FN) = 30 / 40 = 75%`, measuring the percentage of actual positives correctly detected.

## The complete picture

Putting both perspectives side by side:

- **Precision** starts from what the model predicted: `30 / 35` (30 correct hits out of 35 predicted positives).
- **Recall / TPR** starts from what actually existed: `30 / 40` (30 detected hits out of 40 actual positives).

The key takeaway is that both metrics share the exact same numerator — the 30 true positives (TP). The only difference is the denominator: Precision cares about the model's total predictions (35), while Recall cares about the dataset's actual reality (40).
